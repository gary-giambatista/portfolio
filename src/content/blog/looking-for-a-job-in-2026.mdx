<h2 id="story"><span class="num">01</span>My story</h2>

While searching for my next role, I’ve always considered myself lucky to be based in the NYC area, where there are so many jobs. But my search has been tough, and I’m trying to figure out how I can do better. Part of that means coming to terms with the fact that I’ve landed only one interview in nearly half a year of applying for relevant roles. With the pressure on, I prepared rigorously, but I failed to pass because I got stuck solving <a href="https://leetcode.com/problems/minimum-number-of-valid-strings-to-form-target-i/">a very challenging LeetCode problem</a>. Unlucky, or lack of skill? Regardless, I know I need to keep working hard memorizing however many algorithms as it takes and trying again and again for as long as it takes! Unfortunately, “again” hasn’t come yet.

I keep wondering why it’s so hard to get an interview. Is it me, or is it what I’m doing? There are thousands of jobs, right? So I adjusted my strategy to include sending DMs to targeted hiring managers, but so far that has gotten me little more than a lot of unread messages. Nothing seems to be helping me get interviews. What are you all experiencing? Has anyone found a strategy that’s working? The scariest part of all is that, from what I’ve read, <a href="https://www.applypass.com/post/57-000-engineering-interviews-the-interview-threshold-magic-numbers-and-other-lessons">an engineer goes through about 20 interviews, on average, before landing a single offer</a>. At my pace, I'll need to give up on my dream job.

<h2 id="resume"><span class="num">02</span>Is it me or my resume?</h2>

I’ve wondered whether, at least on paper, I just don’t look worth interviewing. Do you ever feel like that too, like the job market doesn’t want you? Recently I met with a recruiter from an agency focusing on highlighting people beyond their resumes to help with this problem. They told me, pretty bluntly, that my resume wasn’t sexy enough to show their clients, and that for someone like me, the best shot was a worldclass portfolio site that gets attention. That confirmed what I’d suspected: on paper, a lot of my peers look stronger than I do, and I am not going to win the battle for attention with my resume alone. So I followed the suggestion and rebuilt my site: <a href="https://gary-giambatista.com/">gary-giambatista.com</a>. Will it help me get interviews? Please let me know what you think. So far, no change yet.

Honestly, is building software really enough to impress people these days? While I was building my site, I sent it to a friend hoping for some encouraging words, and he said, “Cool, but didn’t you just vibe code it?” Talk about a buzzkill. But this is 2026, and he wasn’t wrong. Claude wrote the code, and I directed Claude. We built it as a team, each playing to our strengths: my vision and Claude’s superhuman coding skills. What’s wrong with that? Nothing, except for the fact that anyone can build software like this and that means my marketplace value has plummeted. As much as I have been struggling, maybe this is my fault for assuming that I have value as a software engineer in today’s market where AI models can build businesses the software they need?

For a while, this really got to me. If anyone can build software like this, are my technical skills even worth anything anymore? Did recruiters figure that out before I did? Is that why I can’t get interviews?

Yet, here’s where I’ve landed. Coding was never the hardest part about being a software engineer. Being a good software engineer means asking good questions, figuring out what should be built, how it should feel, and how the pieces fit together, and knowing when something isn’t right yet. Those are the things I love. This is how I can still add value, and lots of it! AI changed how the code gets written. It didn’t remove the need for someone who can see the whole thing and keep pushing until it’s good. I’d love to find a team that sees it that way too.

<h2 id="next"><span class="num">03</span>My next method</h2>

On the bright side, I love building things; that’s actually why I wanted to become a software engineer. And I love it even more with a superhuman buddy by my side. So while I keep looking for the right role, my next stage is already underway. Maybe it will bring a new kind of freedom, or maybe a new kind of pain, but it’s the thing I believe in most right now: build things that solve real problems for people, and let them pay for it if it’s genuinely worth it to them. If employers don’t see the value in my skills yet, maybe someone out there will see it in what I build.

That stage is called Kanji Kaku, an app designed and built by me and my super-intelligent AI friends to teach anyone to write Japanese kanji. You remember each kanji with a little story, write it stroke by stroke with feedback as you go, and review it on a spaced schedule so it sticks. The vision comes from my years of studying kanji and learning from all the great teachers and apps that came before me. If it sounds interesting, please give it a try, and tell me what you think on <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/gary-giambatista/">LinkedIn</a>.
